01 UNDERSTAND THE PROBLEM

The fund can run low.
The checks don't just vanish.

Two things can be true: Social Security faces a serious financing problem, and Social Security will still collect tax revenue.

⌛
WHAT MAY RUN OUT

The savings cushion

For years, Social Security saved extra payroll-tax revenue in Treasury securities. The retirement fund is projected to use up those reserves in late 2032.

Retirement & survivor trust fund (OASI)
↻
WHAT DOESN'T STOP

Money from workers

People will still work and pay Social Security taxes. That incoming money is projected to cover about 78% of scheduled retirement and survivor benefits when the reserves are exhausted.

The program does not stop collecting taxes
⚖
WHAT'S UNDECIDED

What Congress does

Lawmakers can change tax and benefit rules. If they don't, the law does not clearly spell out whether benefits would be reduced on time or paid in full with delays.

The 22% gap is not a law scheduling cuts

THE ROOT CAUSES

Why is the pressure growing?

It's a long-term mismatch, not a single event.

Workers per beneficiary

About 2.6 covered workers for each Social Security beneficiary in 2026, projected to fall to about 2.4 by 2034.

2026 taxable wage limit

Social Security payroll tax applies to earnings up to this annual cap. Earnings above it aren't subject to this particular tax.

The combined program's total costs have exceeded total income since 2021. Reserves have helped cover the difference.

Common questions, clear answers.

Is Social Security going bankrupt? +

No. The retirement fund's reserves are projected to run out, but payroll taxes continue. "Bankrupt" misses that distinction.

Will everyone's check get cut 22%? +

Not necessarily. A 22% financing gap is projected under current law. Congress could prevent it. If Congress does nothing, the exact payment mechanics remain legally unsettled.

What about people already retired? +

Current retirees are exposed to the retirement fund's shortfall too. Starting benefits early does not lock in protection from later changes.

Is disability insurance the same fund? +

No. Disability Insurance (DI) has a separate trust fund, which trustees project will stay solvent through 2100. This is different from SSI, a separate needs-based program.

Did Congress steal the trust fund money? +

The reserves are held in special U.S. Treasury securities—legal claims backed by the government. Redeeming those securities requires the Treasury to provide cash. That creates real budget pressure, but the reserves aren't simply fictitious or "stolen."

Can taxing high earners solve everything? +

It helps substantially. But SSA estimates that taxing all wages above the present ceiling without adding benefit credits closes about 58% of the combined program's 75-year actuarial shortfall—not 100%. Policy details matter.

What's the $100 picture showing?

The Social Security retirement and survivor trust fund holds reserves. The 2026 Trustees Report projects those reserves will be exhausted in the fourth quarter of 2032.

For each $100 in scheduled retirement and survivor benefits at that point, about $78 is projected to be covered by ongoing income and about $22 is a funding shortfall.

The 2034 view assumes Congress changes the law to combine retirement and disability trust funds. That's a hypothetical financing comparison, not what current law permits.

The chart is not a prediction that each person's check will automatically be reduced by exactly that percentage. Congress may act, and the legal payment procedure without action is unsettled.
